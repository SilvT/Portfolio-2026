Energy Switching Tracker

A weeks-long, invisible process, made easy to follow for the customer waiting on it.

Switching energy supplier in the UK can take weeks, and for the customer it all happens in the dark. At Tomato, nobody could walk through the whole process end to end; not customer support, not the engineers.

That lack of visibility is common across the UK energy industry, not specific to any one supplier. Customers moving to Tomato from another supplier waited weeks with no way of knowing what was happening or why, and the milestone emails meant to keep them informed didn't always arrive. When a switch stalled, they contacted support, anxious, about a delay that wasn't always Tomato's to fix.

Tomato brought me in to design a tracker customers could check for themselves. There were two complications: the process wasn't documented anywhere in the company, and I'm not UK-based, so I was learning the British energy market as I went. The goal was to make the process easy to follow and take some of the worry of not knowing off the customer's shoulders.

The Problem: Too Much Silence

Where the gaps were

The wait itself belongs to the industry: the old supplier has to approve the transfer, industry databases have to sync up and meter readings have to be verified. What was under Tomato's control:

✦ Emails that weren't sent or triggered properly.

✦ No way for customers to check status except contacting customer support directly (already saturated most of the time with other queries).

✦ A technical process that lived in pieces across the backend team's heads and nowhere else.

Before designing anything, those pieces had to be glued into one documented process.

Pointing to the Roadblock

Fixing the emails would have improved delivery, but every delay would still have read as silence. So the tracker was meant to be something customers check instead of waiting to be told. It gave them some control back and made them an active part of the process. One magic link, no account, straight to a personalised page wired to the backend and always current. It was designed mobile-first, since a phone is where most customers would check something like this.

When a switch stalled, the tracker showed where and why, and whose move it was: the previous supplier holding it up, something on Tomato's side, or information the customer still needed to provide.

One email, one link. Tomato's email system no longer had to carry the whole experience.

Starting point

I started with a rough three-step example wireframe, a very complex backend diagram and Tomato's brand library in Figma (no brand guidelines). Neither spelled out the error cases (the wireframe had a single "blocked" step), and no copy was provided. Everything else had to be built: a five-step model, both meter journeys, the error categories, the UI, the copy and the motion.

--ux-diagram-My first, rough diagram of the five stages: the backend state, what the customer sees, and what they're told.

--five-steps-The clean version, as built: the steps, the meter fork, and where errors replace the screen.

From 15+ to 5

The backend tracked more than fifteen technical states, and the wireframe I was given had three. I grouped them into five steps a customer can follow without knowing how the industry works. Deciding what to group, what to show and what to leave out is where most of the thinking in this project went.

--playground-Horizontal steppers and colour-block stages, before the vertical dropdown stepper won.

Finding the right layout

I tested several ways of displaying the progress in a UI playground before settling on one:

✦ Dropdown stepper vs. everything visible. How much to show upfront was the core layout question. A progressive stepper lets the current state lead while the rest stays available but quiet, instead of overwhelming an anxious customer with the full process.

✦ Vertical vs. horizontal progress. Vertical won for a mobile-first, single-column read, and it scaled better to the variable number of sub-steps across the two journeys.

--the-fork-Steps 1 to 3 are shared; at step 4 the journey splits into smart and traditional meter.

Two Meters, Two Journeys

Once the switch reached Tomato's side, the journeys split. For smart meter customers the readings were automated, so the tracker showed which stage the connection had reached.

Traditional meters needed the customer to submit a reading by hand, by a set date. Both journeys made a point of showing when the switch was expected to finish and when the first bill would arrive; as everywhere else in the tracker, the priority was keeping the customer as informed as possible.

--switching-errors-Objected, and an error mid-switch.

--technical-and-cancelled-A technical issue, and a switch the customer cancelled.

When It Goes Wrong

Delays aren't all the same, so the errors aren't either. Application errors (wrong MPAN, incorrect address, payment details), switching errors (objections, a supplier blocking the transfer) and technical issues (access problems, system failures) each say what happened and what, if anything, the customer can do about it.

Every error screen keeps the stepper in view, so the customer can still see where in the process it broke.

Trade-off

Error specificity vs. simplicity. Three error categories add complexity, but a generic message would have recreated the original problem: a customer left guessing. Naming the bottleneck and its owner was worth the extra states.

Waiting is the worst part of switching, so that's where I decided the brand's personality could live: tomatoes fall from the top of the screen while data loads, and a small one bounces in the corner while the backend takes its time. They came last, once the journey worked without them.

Beyond the waiting, motion only appears at milestones. A sun with roving eyes turns up when the supply starts: the previous supplier is out of the picture and the energy is now coming from Tomato. It isn't the end, but the hardest part is over, with one company left in the process instead of two. The Tomato logo dances once, at the very end, when all five steps are done; supply, meter and billing are sorted, and the customer can forget the tracker exists.

Trade-off

Playfulness vs. seriousness. A switch involves people's money and their energy supply, and a crossed wire between the old and new supplier can mean being billed twice. So bold animation and playfulness are held back, and never sit next to anything the customer has to act on or vital information (stage details, meter readings, errors). Error states only borrow a few small brand icons, to soften the news without making light of it.

Learnings

Learning the domain from zero. Not being UK-based meant asking the most basic questions about the British energy market. They also turned out to be the questions a customer would ask, which made the five steps easier to name.

A brand library isn't a brand voice. Tomato had components and illustrations but no guidelines, so I had to work out the personality as I used it: where it shows up, and where it stays out.

Mobile-first made the hard calls early. One column and one current step left no room to show everything at once, so what to show and what to hide got decided early instead of being put off.
