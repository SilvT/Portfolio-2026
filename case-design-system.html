No file naming conventions, no shared libraries, no design structure, and no one at the company who yet believed in design systems.

I joined Senapt as its second full designer, and was handed their fourth product line Marketing Management desktop app: a campaigns, leads and channels tracking dashboard. Designed solo opposite a backend developer and a frontend developer seven time-zones ahead in Japan, who read written English but wasn't a fluent speaker. Handing off ambiguously meant every misunderstanding became a huge delay. Across the other product lines, designs were taking four or five review rounds after handoff, and developers sometimes shipped straight to a live product without design sign-off.
My goal was to build this product component-first and systematized from day one, to close that gap before it even opened.

This design system was product specific, yet aimed at the whole company. My first pitch to the CEO of a design system approach overall was turned down outright, yet achieved a tentative yes from the VP of Frontend (though from a pov of bypassing designers in the future). I didn't love the starting point, so instead of fully backing down, I used this app as a Proof of Concept rather than waiting for an approval that the stakeholders weren't ready to give.

Context: The First Drafts

--look-and-feel-screen that got approved

No buy-in? Lead by example

Three already existing product lines, each built by freelancers with no systematic mindset behind them, had accumulated their own disconnected UX, playground and UI files: no naming conventions, no shared libraries, hand-off pages manually kept in sync with the code repo by whoever had time to do so. After the turn down of my pitch and my resolution to use this product as a POC, I set to work on laying some strong foundations and build as stable as a structure as I could.

Scaffolded in Phases, building on the go

I was juggling with product key features desicions, research, ux flows, UI, handoffs, QA, reviews... I was the solo designer in this.

The system was never going to land in one pass, nor be sturdy from the begining, so it didn't try to. Right after getting the approval on the look&feel, I launched into Phase 1: prove the core tokens, type, and a first handful of components, enough to build the dashboard's first complete flow. Phase 2 expanded it: semantic tokens, gradients and elevations, a wider component set that was grown over time out of strict necessity. Phase 3 pushed into solidifing, looking back and reinforce when it had become wobbly.

Delivery and handoffs also followed a set order: components first, screen's sections, full screens, then flows. Every time, changes cascaded predictably because of the architecture underneath.

The semantic layer was wrong twice, in two different ways.

✦ As my first time setting up a design system from zero, newbie errors were to be expected!. The first iteration of semantics was thin, loosely drawn from an outside reference I no longer remember, and token creation quickly skipped past it and component tokens piled up connected straight to primitives. With no dedicated time to build the system properly, about five to six months in, the pattern was obvious and quickly growning to become unmanageable. I gave my frontend developer a heads-up that a large structural change was coming, then scrapped the original semantic layer and rebuilt it from what the screens were actually using. Now that a full ux-flow was completed, I had a much more clearer view of how I, myself, was using colors and values, so extracting the pattern felt more natural.

✦ That rebuilt semantics held for a while, but around month ten to twelve, I noticed something else: patterns repeated across many components, like text or cards having the same hover/focus behaviour everywhere regardless of whether they were nested, colour usage on elevations, disabled statuses on fills/texts/borders/backgrounds... which weren't semantic in the usual sense but weren't component-specific either. Bunching them into the current semantic layer felt conceptually weak, yet a full rebuild didn't make sense. I looked for examples or precedent in other systems and found none, so I outlined a fourth level, sitting between semantic and component and called it post-semantic. The main goal of this layer: to hold these cross-component pattern I was coming across with.

✦ From the get-go dark mode was off the table allowing for a more experimental use of modes in Figma Variables. In a way that felt closer to a prototyping approach, smaller collection, only for component-level tokens, were created on separated collections corresponding with repetitive patterns usages: default/active/focus/hover/disabled, default/pressed/focus/hover/disabled, on/off, xs/s/m/l/xl, primary/secondary/tertiary. This choice kept the system contained in terms of number of variablesand speed up the breakdown for delivery of full ux-flows.

The System

Foundation Architecture

System set up in a traditional 3-tier structure with a weak semantic layer that is strengthen further down the line. Mode usage is used as a tool across a small number of isolated collections, scoped to the component layer, for statuses, alert types and on/off.

A Fourth Level

Post-semantic sits between semantic and component: for patterns that repeated across components (text states, backgrounds, elevations, disabled elements...) without being semantic in the traditional sense the name is exactly what it sounds like: what comes after semantic.

An unsual stack requires a custom solution

This project stack was JavaFX, a language I was unfamiliar with and my research of design systems within it came back empty. It turned out it was also incompatible with every industry standard tool available at the time: Supernova, Storybook and Zeroheight were all ruled out, so the choice was made for me.

Tokens lived in Figma; the developer in Japan needed them as consumable JavaFX-specific CSS. Getting there took two attempts:
✗ First proposal: use Token Studio to get tokens as a JSON file. Denied, asked for CSS files manually sent.
✓ Second proposal: have direct repo access with me pushing from my own machine. This was approved though it meant manually copying and cleaning CSS output by hand for a stretch.

During this manual period, I was convinced a more efficient way existed out there, but knowing my limitations on the tech-side, I reached to a fullstack developer in the company, to help me scope the problem. Together we scaffoled a solution and set it up. The eventual pipeline we ended un building used, initially, Token Studio as the "launchpad", pushing directly to the GitHub repo, where every push will trigger two sets of GitActions: one, will transform into web CSS file (and overwritte previous version), and a consecutive one that will transform the JSON into a JavaFX-specific CSS.

The Buy-in Moment

This particular pipleine was what, unexpectedly, ended up gaining the CEO's attention. He was independently dealing with the same async, cross-language friction with my frontend developer, on an unrelated issue, at the same time I was. In a catchup meeting, I walked him through my delivery tactics as well as this Figma-to-Github pipeline. He was impressed enough and the reaction was immediate: "I want this running on all projects asap!!!!" triggering my official upgrade to Design Systems Lead and greenlight to audit and train the rest of the design team accordingly (of course, while still doing this project full time!)

Now, by this point there were: 7 product lines running on 6 different dev stacks (React, Flutter, Swift, Nuxt, JavaFX,S, Framer). My approach to scaling was "education, not imposition": fill the knowledge gap first, then implement and follow through on rules and guidelines. That took the form of a five-part internal series, Design Bites: the 1.01 sessions, covering foundations, components and terminology, governance, design tokens, and naming systems, alongside hands-on workshops: brand and component self-audits, a shared design-principles exercise, an expert walkthrough on tokens, and first-iteration attempts at the company's own core and semantic tokens.

Reception was outstanding: changed behaviour across every team member but one, followed by individual conversations and requests from designers wanting to work out how the theory applied to their own product's daily reality.

Quirks and Outcomes

~50% Faster Hand-off

Component-first delivery, build the pieces, annotate every state and edge case, assemble screens second, cut hand-off time by roughly half compared to other teams at the company, by informal comparison with other designers rather than controlled measurement. Set against the baseline it replaced: four or five review rounds per handoff, sometimes corrections happening after a product had already gone live.

A Pipeline, Not a Hand-off Ritual

The side effect mattered as much as the fix: the same pipeline that turned token hand-off into a one-click export also unlocked white-label capability for an unrelated, earlier product, which is what first got the CEO's attention, independently of the design system pitch itself. Within roughly five to six months, that became a promotion to Design Systems Lead.

Documentation, Deferred on Purpose

No traditional component documentation existed for most of the build, a deliberate trade-off while the sole consumer of the system was its own author. It held up, but it also meant the system lived in one person's head for longer than was comfortable once the team started to grow, eventually scaling to 8 designers. That gap is part of what the later Design Bites series was built to close: five internal sessions and hands-on workshops, run once governance work began, rather than documentation written as the system was built. With earlier buy-in, component documentation would have started sooner, even lightweight, instead of waiting for a workshop series to catch up.

Learnings

The architectural call to add a fourth level rather than force those patterns into semantic, or rebuild the whole system to accommodate them, was the right one for the problem it solved; it stayed the right call even in hindsight. What I'd revisit is sequencing: with earlier buy-in, component documentation would have started sooner, even lightweight, instead of living in one person's head for the length of the build.

The governance model was still being tested when the company went bankrupt, before the scaling model reached full execution. The structure existed, and the reception from the team it was tested on was genuinely strong; the runway to prove it at full scale didn't.
