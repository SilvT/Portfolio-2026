№02 — Landing screen, reached via a single magic link.

№03 — The tracker on-device, switch progress view.

This tracker replaced an energy supplier's unreliable "we'll email you" updates with a self-service microsite that connects directly to the backend. The job started before any design work: no one at the company, including customer support, could fully explain how switching actually worked, so the first month was spent translating fifteen-plus undocumented backend states into five human-readable stages. The strongest proof of the approach is the documentation it left behind — the prototype became the company's single source of truth for a process it had never written down.

Zooming In: Mapping a System No One Could Explain

Each screen went through the same cycle — pull the backend's raw flow apart, cross-check it against what stakeholders actually wanted, rebuild it as a customer journey, then ship the UI.

№03 — Full cycle: backend diagram → stakeholder draft → customer journey → UI playground

Context: What It Replaced

№04 — The only artefact handed over: a flow diagram built for engineers.

No Documentation, Anywhere

Not for customer service, not for the rest of the company — the switching process existed only in backend code and in the heads of the engineers who wrote it.

№05 — Tomato's brand materials, the constraint to design within.

What It Replaced

Before this, customers got occasional emails at major milestones — and when something stalled, the gap between emails stretched with no way to check status except by calling in.

Strengths

№06 — Magic-link tracker, smart meter journey.

One Link, Not Five Emails

Instead of multiple, often-failed notifications, customers received a single magic link straight to a personalised microsite, directly connected to the backend and always up to date.

№07 — Traditional meter journey, with a manual-reading callout.

Two Journeys, Not One Compromise

Smart and traditional meters needed genuinely different flows. Rather than force a single generic tracker, the design split cleanly in two: automated for smart meters, clear manual-reading prompts for traditional ones.

№08 — Bottleneck view, translated from backend state data.

Naming the Bottleneck

The UI focused on showing exactly where a delay was happening and whose responsibility it was — turning "where's my switch?" support calls into something customers could answer themselves.

№09 — Loading-state animation, frame by frame.

Brand Personality, Under Pressure

To keep Tomato's bold, playful tone alive during data fetches, the design added falling-tomato and bouncing-tomato loading animations — reducing anxiety at the exact moments customers were most likely to feel it.

Quirks

№10 — The journey map, built while learning the market itself.

Learning the Market First

As a non-UK resident, understanding how energy switching legally and operationally works in the UK had to happen before any design decision could be trusted.

№11 — The brief, in practice: one wireframe, no error states.

Building on Someone Else's Sketch

Stakeholders arrived with a fixed vision — a simple vertical stepper — that became the foundation to build on rather than a blank page to design from, error handling and all.

№12 — One of several competing layouts tested for density.

No Single Right Answer

How much information to show upfront, vertical versus horizontal, dropdown versus always-visible — each had a reasonable case, and the final call came down to balancing visual weight against the brand's playful tone.

№13 — Shipped mobile-only, by design, to launch faster.

Mobile-Only Was the Constraint

Desktop was deliberately left out of the first release to get the tracker into customers' hands faster — a constraint that forced sharper prioritisation rather than a compromise absorbed later.
