This platform was built from scratch to replace four departments' worth of manually maintained Excel files with one real-time view of campaigns, leads and channels — and it reshaped the designer who built it. Hired as a mid-level UI designer with no feature definitions to work from, I ended up co-owning product scope and strategy alongside the backend lead, by necessity rather than promotion. The strongest proof of the approach is structural, not decorative: a Facebook-to-CRM pipeline I proposed from user validation now routes support cases automatically, and the systematic async handoff built to survive an 8-hour, cross-language gap with the frontend team became the company's design system — covered in full in its own case study.

The Journey: Building the Plane While Flying It

01

Foundation, Discovery & Early Chaos

Jan–Mar 2024

Outcome: Core architecture validated, look and feel approved.

Competitor research, learning marketing terminology on the fly, and unifying the information architecture across four data sources, with no clear visual direction until month three. The key learning: partnership, early validation and open communication.

02

Systematic Thinking Kicks In

Mar–Apr 2024

Outcome: Reusability became the only way forward.

Frontend onboarded with a single approved screen, no component library precedent, a stack that didn't support standard tooling, and async collaboration across timezones and a language barrier. Short-term fixes weren't sustainable.

03

The Product Stopped Shifting

Jul–Nov 2024

Outcome: Foundations held; new features could grow on top.

Leads dashboard shipped, component library v1 landed with tokens, and user validation surfaced the Facebook DM bottleneck that became the CRM pipeline proposal. I took over sprint coordination with no PM in place, and a second backend developer joined to support the growing architecture.

04

Advancing Features

Dec 2024–May 2025

Outcome: Systematic delivery held as feature complexity increased.

Campaigns dashboard shipped, the Facebook-to-CRM pipeline went live, and a unified inbox merged social media with customer support. Handoff scaled through detailed Figma annotations, microcopy in every prototype, and muted video walkthroughs for the language gap. Documentation replaced constant alignment.

05

Proving the System

Jun–Oct 2025

Outcome: The proof of concept unlocked company-wide buy-in.

MVP shipped, the Figma-to-Git pipeline validated at ~90% design-to-code accuracy, and the CEO approved expanding the design system company-wide. A Design Ops team formed, and I trained five designers on the systematic handoff method, the foundations built here became the company's design system.

Strengths

№06 — Co-ownership decision framework, Senapt LTD.

Partnership Over Hierarchy

Rather than compete for the CEO's attention, the backend lead and I split by domain and shared everything strategic — a decision framework that held for the full eighteen months.

№07 — Early problem/solution sketch, co-developed with backend lead.

Spotting the Real Problem

Social media managers were being pulled into support conversations arriving as Facebook messages, with no connection to the CRM. I proposed using the conversation itself to identify and route support cases automatically.

№09 — Handoff documentation reaching ~90% design-to-code accuracy.

Documentation Over Distance

Working async across an 8-hour gap and a language barrier with the frontend team in Japan meant documentation had to replace conversation — Figma annotations for every state, prototypes with real microcopy, and muted video walkthroughs for complex flows.

Quirks

№10 — The collaboration model drawn up to cover the gap.

No Product, No UX

There was no product team, no UX function, and no one deciding what "done" meant for any given section — that ambiguity had to be absorbed before any design work could start.

№11 — The entirety of available "prior art" at month one.

One Screen to Onboard On

The frontend team was onboarded with only one approved screen to work from, on a framework — Java FX — with thin documentation and no component library precedent to borrow against.

№12 — A revised sketch, after the first pass didn't hold.

Foundations Kept Shifting

Early decisions kept getting re-tested as the foundations were still being poured. Priorities shifted mid-build, assumptions broke, and not every idea that looked solid on paper survived contact with the backend's constraints.

№13 — Solo UX pass, social inbox interaction patterns.

No Buffer, No Team

Acting as a one-person team covering UI, UX, systems and parts of project management meant no buffer between a decision and its consequences — the process was, at times, genuinely chaotic.
