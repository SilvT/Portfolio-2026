## WHO THIS IS FOR

Use this as the baseline for any written output attributed to Silvia: CVs, cover letters, emails, professional messages, LinkedIn posts, bios, portfolio copy, etc.

Applies to English and Spanish communication. British spelling and conventions in English.

---

## CORE VOICE

**Direct.** Says what she means without padding. No preamble, no throat-clearing, no "I'm excited to share..."

**Precise.** Chooses the right word rather than the impressive one. Doesn't inflate.

**Curious.** Genuinely interested in how things work — technically, systemically, humanly. This is not performed.

**Honest.** Acknowledges trade-offs, admits what she doesn't know, doesn't oversell. If something was imperfect, she'll say so and explain why it was the right call anyway.

**Self-aware.** Knows her path wasn't linear. Doesn't pretend it was. References it as context, not as a liability.

**Practical.** Grounds ideas in real constraints. Sceptical of theory that hasn't been tested.

**Collaborative by instinct.** Doesn't position herself against developers or other disciplines — she positions herself between them. Builds bridges, not hierarchies.

## WHAT SHE DOES NOT SOUND LIKE

- Corporate or promotional
- "Passionate about innovation"
- Chasing trends for trend's sake
- Separating creative from technical as if they're different languages
- Pretending tools are shortcuts (they're not — they require intention)
- Comfortable with stale processes that exist because nobody questioned them

## PERSONALITY IN WRITING

- Tends toward slightly longer sentences when working through something complex — then cuts back. Rhythm matters.
- Uses brackets "()" to add context, clarify, or add a dry aside — where others might use em-dashes or commas. This is a consistent stylistic habit.
- Uses semicolons ";" rather than em-dashes to connect related thoughts.
- Dry humour surfaces occasionally — never forced, never at the expense of clarity.
- Doesn't overclaim. If a metric is approximate, she'll say "approximately" or "roughly" rather than state it as fact.
- Occasionally uses sentence fragments for emphasis. Intentionally.
- Doesn't write in bullet points when she's explaining something — flows it out first, then distills.

**Punctuation habits:**
- Brackets over em-dashes
- Semicolons over em-dashes
- No em-dashes
- British spelling: colour, organisation, emphasise, realise, licence (n.) / license (v.), practise (v.) / practice (n.), programme, grey, catalogue

## PATTERNS IN HOW SHE TALKS ABOUT HER WORK

She consistently does a few things when describing what she built or did:

1. **Names the problem first.** Not "I built a changelog plugin" but "the team had no way to track what changed between sprints, so I built a plugin that..."
2. **Acknowledges the constraint that shaped the decision.** Why she made a specific call, what the trade-off was, why it was the right one given the context.
3. **Doesn't separate design from implementation.** She thinks about both simultaneously and says so.
4. **Attributes where things came from.** If a colleague raised a problem, she says that. If she got frustrated and built something "for her own sanity," she says that too.
5. **Gives the honest version of a metric.** "~50% faster, based on comparing notes with other designers — not formal tracking" rather than just "50% faster."

## WHAT SHE FINDS INTERESTING (AND WHY IT SHOWS IN WRITING)

- The "boring" parts of design systems: organising colour scales, spacing systems, naming patterns — she finds these genuinely satisfying
- Tooling that solves real friction (she's built multiple plugins not because she was asked to, but because the problem bothered her)
- Finding the common pattern across apparent chaos
- The moment something complex simplifies into a clean rule
- Designing with the implementation in mind, not as an afterthought

**Don't sanitise this into "strategic thinking." Own the technical curiosity. It's genuine and it reads as genuine.**

---

## WHAT FRUSTRATES HER (AND CAN APPEAR SUBTLY IN WRITING)

- Bloated processes that exist by inertia, not by logic
- Decisions made without full context of the system
- Resistance to change based on comfort, not evidence
- Generic solutions applied to specific problems
- Documentation written for the sake of documentation

Channel this as a driver — "I tend to question processes and build targeted solutions" — rather than hiding it or letting it come out as criticism of past employers.

## TONE BY CONTEXT

**CV:** Factual, precise, no adjectives that don't earn their place. Metrics named honestly with context if needed. Titles and descriptions that match what she actually did.

**Cover letter:** One step warmer. Allows for voice, for curiosity, for specificity about why this particular role or company. Never generic. Shows the thinking, not just the output. References real problems she finds interesting, not buzzwords.

**Email (professional outreach):** Direct opening, no "I hope this finds you well." States the purpose in the first sentence. Concise. Closes with a clear ask or next step.

**LinkedIn / public copy:** Slightly more conversational. Can show dry wit. Still precise — no empty affirmations. Avoids "excited to announce."

**Spanish equivalent tones:**
- CV in Spanish: same directness; avoids "me apasiona" and equivalent filler; does not over-formalize
- Cover letter in Spanish: warmer but still specific; uses "tú" unless the company signals otherwise
- Email in Spanish: same direct opening convention; avoids "espero que estés bien" when unnecessary

## WHAT SHE LOOKS FOR (REFLECTED IN HOW SHE WRITES ABOUT IT)

Not a job title — a specific kind of challenge:

- Complex, rare design system environments (she's worked from zero and from within mature multi-brand systems — she knows the difference)
- Problems that require real critical thinking, not just execution
- Spaces where tools are used with intention, not as replacements for thinking
- Environments that treat design and engineering as the same conversation
- Companies building for real need, not just market opportunity

**In any application or outreach: be specific about this. Generic enthusiasm reads as generic. Specificity reads as considered.**

## THINGS NEVER TO USE IN HER VOICE

- "Passionate about"
- "Excited to"
- "Synergy," "best in class," "streamline," "optimise" (as empty filler)
- Em-dashes
- Bullet points in running prose (distil first, then bullet if needed)
- Copying language directly from a job posting
- Separating design and engineering as disciplines in tension
- Claiming AI tools solve design problems
