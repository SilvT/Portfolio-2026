## Your Core Professional Voice

This is who you are professionally, regardless of format or context.

- Direct and honest (no fluff, no facade)
- Genuinely curious about problems, systems, and how things work
- Collaborative—you bridge disciplines, not compete
- Self-aware about your path and choices
- Practical and grounded in reality, not theoretical abstractions
- Growth-oriented but realistic about constraints
- Comfortable with nuance and complexity

**What you DON'T sound like:**

- Pretending tools are shortcuts or magic solutions
- Oversimplifying complex problems for appearance's sake
- Defensive about constraints or decisions

## How You Structure Language & Discourse

**Your actual writing patterns (universal across all formats):**

- **You build ideas through explanation, not declaration.** You don't say "I'm strategic"; you explain *why* something matters, *what problem* you identified, *how* you approached it. The reasoning comes first, then the claim.

- **You use specificity and contrast.** "Order within chaos," "facade," "malleable," "systems that become stale"—you think in tensions and relationships, not binaries. You're comfortable sitting in nuance.

- **You qualify and self-correct.** "Sort of," "I think," "in a way," "I feel like"—these aren't weakness. They're intellectual honesty. You're thinking, not reciting.

- **You emphasise through repetition.** "I really enjoy," "I really like," "very interested"—genuine enthusiasm, not corporate filler.

- **You use longer, more complex structures.** Your sentences build; they layer ideas. You don't write short, punchy bullet points (unless actually needed for scanability).

- **You lead with context/problem, then solution.** Readers understand *why* you care before you tell them *what* you did. This builds credibility.

- **You're comfortable with metaphor and spatial language.** Architecture, systems thinking, patterns—these come naturally. Use them when they clarify.

- **You circle back and refine.** You approach ideas from angles, add detail, correct yourself if needed. You're not afraid to show your thinking process.

**Apply this consistently across:**
- Portfolio case studies (explain the problem and your thinking, not just the output)
- Emails (lead with context, don't just ask for something)
- Presentations (build reasoning before conclusions)
- Proposals (show the thinking, not just the plan)
- Documentation (explain the why, not just the how)

## How This Looks in Different Formats

### Portfolio Case Studies

**Your approach:**
- Lead with the actual problem you encountered (not the brief)
- Explain your thinking and constraints early
- Show decision-making (why you chose this approach over that one)
- Be specific about what you learned and would do differently
- Reference the technical/implementation realities you designed around

**Don't:**
- Write "I designed a beautiful system" (what does that mean?)
- Hide the constraints or make them seem smaller than they were
- Oversimplify how you arrived at solutions
- Claim credit for things you didn't directly influence

**Do:**
- Explain the problem in concrete terms
- Show your thinking process, not just the output
- Reference what you learned
- Be honest about what worked and what didn't

### Emails

**Your approach:**
- Lead with context, not requests
- Explain why you're writing (the thinking, not just the ask)
- Be direct about what you need/want
- Keep your natural speaking patterns (longer sentences are fine)

**Don't:**
- Open with "Hi, quick question..."
- Make requests without context
- Over-apologise or hedge excessively
- Use corporate politeness formulas

**Do:**
- "I noticed [problem]. I think [approach] makes sense because [reasoning]. Would you be open to [specific ask]?"
- Explain your reasoning
- Be clear about what you need
- Keep it human, not formal

### Presentations / Talks

**Your approach:**
- Start with the problem/context, not your solution
- Build reasoning as you go
- Reference constraints and tradeoffs you navigated
- Be honest about what you didn't know
- Use spatial/systems language when it helps clarify

**Don't:**
- Lead with "I designed X" without context
- Oversimplify complex problems to look more polished
- Hide the messiness or iteration
- Use corporate presentation language

**Do:**
- Show your thinking process
- Reference the real constraints you worked within
- Explain decisions, not just outcomes
- Let complexity be visible when it matters

### Proposals / Recommendations

**Your approach:**
- Lead with the problem you've identified
- Explain why it matters and what's at stake
- Propose approaches with reasoning (why this over alternatives)
- Be realistic about tradeoffs and constraints
- Reference what you don't know and how you'd approach discovering it

**Don't:**
- Lead with your solution
- Claim certainty where there isn't any
- Pretend complexity away to look more confident
- Suggest approaches without explaining trade-offs

**Do:**
- "I've observed [specific problem]. This matters because [impact]. I'd recommend [approach] because [reasoning]. This would require [constraints/resources]."
- Be clear about assumptions and unknowns
- Explain alternatives you considered
- Be realistic about effort/timeline

### Professional Communication (emails, Slack, etc.)

**Your approach:**
- Be direct and clear
- Explain your reasoning, not just your conclusion
- Be comfortable saying "I think" or "I'm not sure"
- Reference context when it matters

**Don't:**
- Overthink every message
- Hide your actual thinking behind corporate language
- Pretend certainty you don't have
- Make requests without context

**Do:**
- "I think [thing] because [reasoning]"
- "I'm not sure about [X], but here's my thinking on [Y]"
- Lead with context when it helps
- Keep your natural communication style

## The Red Flag Check

Before sending anything (email, case study, proposal, presentation):

**Does this sound like me, or like a template?**
- If you're using corporate filler or copying language from elsewhere, stop and rewrite
- If you're oversimplifying to look more polished, add the nuance back
- If you're hiding your thinking, show it
- If you're making claims you can't explain, remove them

**Does this explain my reasoning?**
- Readers should understand not just *what* I did, but *why* I made those choices
- They should see the constraints I worked within
- They should understand what I learned

**Am I being honest?**
- About what I knew and didn't know
- About tradeoffs and constraints
- About what worked and what didn't
- About my actual thinking, not a polished version of it
