The brief was 20 minutes long: two hand-drawn sketches, three section names (Campaigns, Leads, Channels) and nothing written down. I was hired as a UI designer; a month in, someone had to decide what actually went inside each of those names.

There was no product team and no UX function to hand that to. The backend developer needed direction as much as I did, so the two of us ended up co-owning scope. We split along technical lines and shared the strategic calls: they owned architecture, data models and APIs; I owned design, research, specs and QA. The marketing lead joined as end user and domain expert, and the CEO got results-based check-ins from both of us.

Eighteen months later, a three-person team had a production-ready MVP.

The Problem: Fragmented Data, Blind Decisions

--before-Campaign tracking as it was: spreadsheets, updated by hand.

--the-brief-The two sketches the project started from.

--lead-sources-Seven lead sources, most of them untracked.

Where the data lived

Marketing Management is Senapt's own software; Tomato Energy, a UK energy supplier, was its first client and where it was tested. Its marketing team tracked campaign performance across several manually maintained Excel files, updated when someone had time, for roughly 14K customers (with growth to 24K projected over the following year). Leads arrived through six or more channels, and most of them weren't tracked at all:

✦ Telesales, tracked by a different department.

✦ Social media, untracked; complaints in the comments never reached Customer Care.

✦ The website's own quotation tool, untracked.

✦ Door-to-door campaigns and live events, untracked.

What I started with

No component library or pattern precedent, a stack (JavaFX) with thin documentation and little mature tooling, and a frontend developer working fully async from Japan, seven hours ahead. For the first four or five months I also had no direct access to the marketing department.

The Shift: Becoming Co-Owners

--ia-growth-How the structure grew: from the three sections in the brief to the full information architecture, with a pass down the Leads branch.

I was given the three section names and the sketches. Everything else had to be built: the information architecture, the flows, the UI, the component library it was built on, and the specs frontend worked from.

Nobody was going to define the product for us, so the decision was how to define it ourselves without stepping on each other. Backend and I agreed a simple rule: scope was decided between the two of us, from what the marketing lead needed and what the timeline allowed, and design approach and system decisions sat with me. Anything strategic went to the CEO as a result to react to, not an open question.

In practice I proposed features from research and real workflows, and backend checked them against the data, the architecture and the constraints. Many ideas didn't survive that; the ones that did became the roadmap, with backing in place long before they reached an executive review.

Two co-owners, one rule. Scope was shared; the system was mine.

What This Actually Looked Like

--campaign-lifecycle-Campaign statuses and actions, with the open questions kept as notes.

--wireframes-The two-step campaign wizard and the campaigns dashboard.

From names to flows

Competitor research came first, cross-checked with the marketing lead, plus an LLM to get up to speed on marketing terminology I didn't have. Campaigns started as a status-and-actions map full of open questions ("can a campaign be extended without adding budget?"), which became the basis for scoping conversations rather than a finished answer.

The wireframes then turned into a step-by-step map of the wizard, with every input and the rule behind it: the channel type decides whether a platform is needed, a free channel needs no budget input, and anything still unresolved (which products a campaign could be tied to) stayed marked as an open question.

--wizard-logic-Every input in the wizard and the rules behind it: channel type decides platform and name, a free channel skips the budget.

--with-backend-Marketing objectives from the code check to the shipped step, with the label logic for "Select all".

Design and data model in the same diagram

Once backend had a model, I mapped it field by field against the UX, so every input in the campaign wizard (audience, targets, marketing objectives) had a matching field in the code before it reached frontend.

Trade-off

Quick fixes vs. reusable components. With frontend waiting on screens, one-off solutions were faster in the moment, but they didn't survive the next feature. Reusable components cost more up front and became the only way to keep pace.

--create-campaign-The six-step wizard, from campaign details to the new row in the list.

--lead-detail-One lead from a campaign: property, history, cohort and conversion.

Where a campaign ends up

Every campaign's result lands in Leads, so the lead page closes the loop: the campaign that brought the person in, their property, client history and cohort sit together, with actions (surveys, quality checks) at the bottom where the marketing lead's workflow ended.

The Feature Nobody Asked For

--posts-wireframe-The posts grid, annotated with every action the API exposes.

--posts-The same grid in the UI.

--post-card-One post card in its four states, as a placeholder component and with real content.

Designing for the API review

Getting social data in meant designing around Facebook's API review first and users second. Early dashboards had to be cut back to what the review would approve, and two versions were kept in parallel so work on features still waiting for access wasn't lost.

Once access came through, the marketing lead validated the inbox and comments views, which surfaced two gaps: nobody owned following up on a given conversation, and the social media manager was absorbing support traffic that belonged to Customer Care. The first became an internal tagging and assignment system.

--connect-to-business-From a Facebook message to a matched customer in the CRM.

--in-the-inbox-The contract number arrives, the customer is identified, and one click hands the case to Customer Care.

From a complaint to a ticket

Before this, a support message on Facebook was emailed to Customer Care by hand; Care located the customer and opened a ticket, while the social team replied separately on Facebook. I proposed using the conversation itself instead: an automatic reply asks for a contract number, the number is checked against the CRM, and the inbox marks the conversation as a support case. One action then opens a CRM ticket with the full message history, and the customer is told their issue has been passed on. I validated it with backend, marketing and a Customer Care contact before taking it to the CEO, so it arrived as a worked solution with a draft architecture.

A Handoff Built for Async

--handoff-Posts grid, list view and states, annotated for frontend.

With frontend seven hours ahead, a question asked at the end of my day was answered the next morning at best. So the specs had to answer it first:

✦ Every component annotated with its tokens, and every state (default, hover, pressed, focus) drawn out.

✦ Prototypes with the final microcopy, so nothing was left to guess.

✦ Error states and edge cases documented alongside the happy path.

✦ Silent video walkthroughs for complex flows, which worked across the language barrier.

✦ A backend review before each handoff, so endpoint names matched.

That level of annotation took longer up front, and it's where the review-round figures in the outcomes come from.

Trade-off

Documentation vs. live alignment. Each spec took longer to hand over, but it replaced the constant back-and-forth that a seven-hour gap and a language barrier made too slow.

Look and Feel

--first-approved-The first screen signed off.

--identity-A quick, rough identity; never a full style guide.

There was no visual direction to start from. I put together broad mood-board explorations; after two or three rounds of feedback, the CEO shared their own visual references, which finally gave us something concrete to approve against.

The same meeting that approved the look and feel brought frontend in to start immediately, on a library that held rough brand colours, rough text styles, a bento-box card layout and a borrowed icon set. The identity grew from there.

Outcomes

--the-product-Home, leads, campaigns, interactions and analytics: the main screens of the MVP.

A production-ready MVP in 18 months, built by a three-person team. Digital and traditional marketing were visible in one place for the first time, and reporting moved from a two-week lag to real time. Events got a capture tool writing to the same database, social messages with certain keywords came in as leads, and telesales were cross-referenced against the sales CRM for a rough read on funnel conversion.

The handoff process cut QA and design review rounds by roughly 90% compared with other projects at the company (around one round for every 8 or 9 the rest of the team needed). Builds of my designs ran roughly twice as fast as a result. Both are peer comparisons, not tracked data.

The CEO had started out sceptical of design-system talk; the system got their buy-in as a proof of concept around month 5 or 6, and was adopted across the company at month 15. The product itself ended up in investor conversations, as an asset with white-label potential, and on the company's booth at developer events, neither of which was in the brief.

Learnings

The MVP only reached user testing at the end of the project, so there's no usage data to show yet. I'd get the marketing team testing earlier, on the Leads dashboard rather than the finished product, since for the first months every workflow decision went through one person. I'd also have tracked the review rounds and build times properly from the start; the figures above come from comparing notes with the rest of the design team.

Scope is a design decision. Nobody handed over a definition of "Campaigns", so deciding what went into it, and what waited for v2, became part of the design work. Most of what I now call product thinking started as answering that question with backend, one section at a time.

Validation from daily collaborators counts for more than sign-off. Backend checked feasibility, the marketing lead checked workflows and a Customer Care contact checked the pipeline, so ideas reached the CEO already tested. Executive review stopped being where things were decided and became where they were confirmed.

Written handoffs scale better than meetings. The annotation habit started as a workaround for a seven-hour gap and a language barrier. It ended up as the part of the project other designers were asked to learn from.

Responsibility comes before the title. I joined as a mid-level UI designer with no product experience; priorities shifted, early decisions broke, and there was no buffer between a call and its consequences. What stayed with me is learning to keep moving without the full picture, and growing into the role by doing it rather than waiting for the label first.
