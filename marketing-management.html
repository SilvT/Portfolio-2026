No product team, no UX, no feature definitions; three section names and a backend developer who also needed direction.

I was hired with a background in UI design. One month in, I was faced with three section names, no feature definitions, and a backend developer who was as lost as me, and with a CEO too busy to answer our questions (what actually goes in "Campaigns"? How deep does each section go? What's v1, what's v2? Who decides?).

There was no product or UX team to ask. Backend and I became co-owners of scope because nobody else was around and it needed to be done. We split along technical lines but shared every strategic call: they owned architecture, data models and APIs; I owned design, research, specs and ux. The marketing lead, brought in as the product's end user and domain expert, gave us validation and workflow knowledge; the CEO got results-based check-ins from both of us. For the first few months we also weren't allowed to talk to the marketing department directly; that restriction lifted around month 4–5.

The Problem

The marketing team was flying blind. Campaign performance lived across several manually maintained Excel files, updated when someone had time, with roughly 14K customers on the books and growth to 24K projected over the next year. Data entry was manual across 6+ touchpoints: live events, traditional media, four-plus social platforms, review and forum sites, a custom quotation tool on the website, telesales. None of it talked to the others. Leads came in fragmented: some via telesales (tracked by a different department), some via social media (not tracked at all), some via the website's own quotation tool (not tracked either), some from door-to-door campaigns and events (also untracked). Social media comments and complaints weren't reaching Customer Care.

Going in:

✦ An engineer-first CEO, initially dismissive of design-systems thinking.

✦ No component library or pattern precedent.

✦ An uncommon stack (JavaFX) with no mature tooling or references.

✦ A frontend developer onboarded with a single approved UI screen (look&feel).

✦ Collaboration fully async across a 7-hour gap and a language barrier.

✦ No PM, no UX, no defined MVP, no defined competitor set.

✦ Scattered data-entry points across 4 departments(marketing, sales, customer care, social media).

What This Actually Does

Campaigns

Creation (a six-step flow pushing straight to Google Ads once approved), live performance against spend and lead volume.

Leads

Each contact from first touch through a quality score, a cohort profile and lifetime value, with an automatic satisfaction survey once a deal closes.

Interactions

The social inbox: comments and messages from Facebook (Instagram and TikTok tabs scaffolded for later), assignable, flaggable for support, filterable and archivable.

Analytics

Cost-per-lead, CAC by product and by campaign, ad performance down to best time of day, and a CPC-to-CTR efficiency view, the whole reason the project existed.

Proving the System

Month 15

MVP ready for user testing; handoff approach greenlit company-wide.

Competitor research came first, self-driven and cross-checked with the marketing lead: flows, features, look and feel, plus using an LLM to get up to speed on marketing terminology I didn't have. Look and feel had no direction from the CEO at all, so I put together broad mood-board explorations; after two or three rounds of pushback, the CEO sent over his own direct visual references, which finally gave the project a clear direction to approve against. The same meeting that approved the look and feel brought the frontend developer in and asked him to start immediately, on a library that at that point held only rough brand colours, rough text styles, a bento-box card layout and a borrowed material-symbols icon set.

That stage was genuinely stressful, worth naming rather than smoothing over: no product, no UX, no team to lean on; co-owning the roadmap and the architecture conversation with backend despite never having done that before; building a design system foundation because nobody else would, on top of a look and feel that was itself still rough; frontend waiting on deliverables while I was still unsure of the features, the information architecture, or half the marketing concepts I was designing for.

Two months in, the CEO asked for something unrelated: a lead-capturing tablet tool for an upcoming door-to-door campaign, needed in two weeks, built in Nuxt.js rather than JavaFX, with an explicit instruction that it carry no visual connection to this product's brand. It shipped on time, fed into the same backend being built for Marketing Management, and nobody (including the CEO) loved the UI, but it worked; it was later deprecated once the website's own quotation tool started covering that same lead-capture role.

Async by Necessity

Every handoff had to survive a 7-hour gap and a language barrier, so the process itself became the product. A "ready for dev" tag from design and a "mark as completed" tag from development replaced any need to ask whether something had shipped. Annotations covered component behaviour, data origin and pending-feature notes, sorted by category, so nothing needed a live explanation to make sense. Backend was never comfortable working inside Figma, so anything that needed sketching together happened in Miro instead.

Designing Against Someone Else's API

Getting social data in meant designing around Facebook's own API approval process rather than users first.

The Interactions build, from early wireframes through the tagging system that came out of marketing-lead validation.

Early dashboard drafts had to be revised and resubmitted specifically to match what Facebook's API would actually grant access to, stripping out anything that might read as more than the review process was built to approve. Two versions were kept in parallel so work already done on features still waiting for access wasn't lost.

Once access came through, the inbox and comments views went to the marketing lead for validation, which surfaced a real workflow gap: nobody owned following up on a given conversation, and the social media manager was absorbing support and complaint traffic that belonged elsewhere in the company. The fix for the first became an internal tagging and assignment system for conversations; the fix for the second became a pipeline connecting flagged conversations straight into the company's CRM, so a complaint arriving over Facebook could reach Customer Care without being relayed by hand.

Outcome

Fifteen months got the MVP to production-ready, built by a three-person team. Backend and I got CEO buy-in on the design system as a proof of concept around month 5–6; the buy-in to expand that approach into the rest of the company came later, around month 15.

The systematic, heavily annotated handoff process cut QA and design review rounds after development by roughly 90% against other projects at the company, by their own comparison rather than formal measurement. Reporting moved from a two-week lag to real time. The product ended up used in investor conversations, discussed there as an asset with white-label potential, and shown at company booths during developer events, neither of which was the brief when the project started.

Learnings

I joined as a mid-level UI designer with no product or leadership experience. This project didn't leave room to learn that gradually; scoping, designing, shipping and revisiting all happened at once, with no clean handoff between them and no one else to default decisions to when something broke.

Not everything held. Priorities shifted, the door-to-door tablet detour ate two weeks nobody had budgeted, and acting as a one-person design team across UI, UX, systems and parts of project management meant there was no buffer between a decision and its consequences. What stuck wasn't a single feature or metric; it was learning to keep moving without the full picture, and growing into the responsibility by doing it rather than waiting for a title to arrive first.
